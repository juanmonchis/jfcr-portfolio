Designing trust at the hardest moment to earn it

Aury is a conversational AI designed to support people dealing with stress, anxiety, and burnout — developed by clinical psychologists in Germany, grounded in CBT, ACT, and mindfulness, and built to strict European data and regulatory standards. Its ambition is significant: to give access to psychological support to the billion people who currently have none.

The design challenge was unusually high-stakes. Mental health is among the most sensitive contexts a product can operate in. Users arrive at Aury when they’re already struggling — tired, anxious, sometimes in genuine distress. The design has to earn trust before it earns engagement. A single jarring interaction — the wrong tone, an unclear prompt, a UI that feels clinical and cold — and the user closes the app and doesn’t come back.

“In mental health, the design is the therapy. If the interface feels unsafe, the conversation never happens.”

My previous work in healthcare products gave me a framework for this kind of constraint: you’re not just designing usability, you’re designing emotional safety. Every decision — colour, typography, copy, interaction pattern — is a signal that either builds or erodes the user’s confidence that this space is for them.

Everything, from the logo to the conversation

Working directly with the two founders, I owned the full design surface of the product — there was no handoff, no second designer, no agency. Every creative decision came through this collaboration.

A brand that had to feel safe before it felt smart

The mental health space is split between two visual extremes: sterile clinical whites that feel like a waiting room, and over-saturated wellness pastels that feel patronising. Neither was right for Aury.

Aury needed to feel like a companion — warm enough to be approachable, restrained enough to feel trustworthy, and distinctive enough to stand out from both the clinical and the wellness-app aesthetic. The founders had built the product with clinical rigour and genuine care; the brand needed to communicate both simultaneously.

The three qualities the brand had to hold at once mapped directly from the product’s own principles: calm and safe (for users arriving in distress), scientifically credible (for B2B partners and health insurers), and human and warm (because the whole point is connection, not efficiency).

“Aury” — from “aura” — carries connotations of presence, atmosphere, and emotional resonance. The brand needed to honour that: not loud, not urgent, but unmistakably there. A companion with a personality, not a tool with a function.

Designing a conversation that people trust with their worst days

The core of Aury is a conversation. Unlike a standard chatbot UI — which is mostly a solved problem — a mental health conversation UI carries specific requirements that most chat interfaces aren’t designed for.

The interaction model had to feel guided but not scripted, supportive but not saccharine, and structured enough to deliver clinical value while remaining flexible enough to respond to what a user actually needs in that moment.

Key interaction decisions

01

Pacing over efficiency. Standard UX optimises for task completion speed. A mental health conversation optimises for the opposite — space to think, breathe, and respond honestly.

02

Structured sessions with open check-ins. Two distinct modes: structured 8-session course content and free-form check-ins. The UI handles both without making either feel like a compromise.

03

Trust signals embedded in the UI. ISO certification, GDPR compliance, EU hosting — surfaced as emotionally relevant context integrated at the moments users need them.

04

Crisis escalation as a design pattern. Aury scans for crisis indicators. The UI shifts register immediately: warm but direct, clear about limits, specific about where to get real help.

05

Honesty about what Aury is. Aury is not a therapist. These limits are designed into the product’s voice, onboarding, and escalation patterns — communicating them clearly is itself a trust signal.

A site doing two completely different jobs at once

Aury’s marketing website has two separate audiences with fundamentally different needs: consumers who are anxious about whether this is safe to trust with their mental health, and B2B partners — health insurers, corporate HR platforms, healthcare companies — evaluating a white-label AI infrastructure investment.

These audiences need different information, different tones, and different conversion flows. The site architecture had to serve both without compromising either.

The consumer side

The consumer homepage leads with emotional utility — “Fühle dich besser” (Feel better) — not features or technical capability. The design prioritises use-case recognition over product explanation: rather than describing what Aury does, it presents the situations users are already in and lets them self-select. Safety and data protection are surfaced as emotional reassurance, not compliance footnotes.

The B2B side

The B2B platform page is a different product. Its audience is evaluating clinical credibility, regulatory compliance, integration feasibility, and commercial terms. The design reflects that: certification badges, a clear four-step go-live process, and industry-specific use case modules for health insurers, healthcare companies, and corporate clients.

The four-week go-live claim is a significant commercial differentiator. The design makes it visible and credible — not buried in a features list, but laid out as a concrete, step-by-step process that any technical stakeholder can evaluate.

Designing within one of Europe’s most demanding regulatory environments

Aury operates at the intersection of multiple regulatory frameworks — GDPR, the EU AI Act, ISO 27001, ZPP (Germany’s prevention course certification), and a roadmap toward MDR medical device classification. For any of these to be meaningful to users and partners, they had to be communicated through design, not just documented in legal pages.

My healthcare background informed how these were handled in the design. In regulated health products, compliance isn’t a footnote — it’s a primary user need. The design integrates these signals at the exact moments they’re needed: onboarding, the B2B platform page, and the safety page.

In health products, the safety information is product design. The page that explains what Aury cannot do — crisis escalation, explicit limits, emergency contacts — isn’t a legal disclaimer. It’s a critical part of the user experience.

What designing for mental health changes about how you work

Trust is the primary UX metric
Before engagement, before retention — users in mental health contexts are asking one question: is this safe? My healthcare background meant I started from that frame, not arrived at it.
Designing limits is as important as designing features
Aury’s safety page and crisis escalation patterns aren’t edge cases — they’re core product design. A mental health tool that obscures its limits is dangerous.
Conversation UI is a different design discipline
There are no layouts to compose. The design is entirely in pacing, register, voice, and the logic of what comes next — mapped from the therapeutic structure.
Sole designer, two founders: fast and high-pressure
No design review, no second opinion. The discipline of being rigorous when no process forces you to be is something I’d actively seek out again.
B2C and B2B require deliberate context-switching
The consumer app and B2B platform share a brand but speak to completely different audiences. Designing both coherently required maintaining two distinct mental models.
